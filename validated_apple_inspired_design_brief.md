# Apple-Inspired Website Design Brief

## Primary goal
Create an Apple-inspired website that feels exceptionally clear, calm, premium, spacious, and easy to understand. The page should communicate one idea at a time and should be specific enough that an AI website generator can execute it without inventing a different visual language.

## Mandatory design principles
- Use strong visual hierarchy, generous whitespace, restrained typography, premium imagery, and minimal decoration.
- Give every major section one dominant message and one dominant visual.
- Prefer short headlines and short supporting copy over paragraphs of explanation.
- Use a mostly neutral palette: white, soft gray, near-black, and one restrained accent when needed.
- Use light and dark sections intentionally to create rhythm.
- Keep the interface quiet. Navigation, controls, borders, and shadows should never compete with the main message.
- Do not default to generic SaaS patterns such as dense card grids, neon gradients, glassmorphism, floating badges, excessive icons, or decorative dashboard widgets.
- Do not imitate Apple branding, logos, product names, or proprietary page copy. Use the design principles, not a literal clone.

## Visual hierarchy
The reading order must be obvious at a glance:
Hero statement → supporting sentence → primary action → product visual → next story.

Each section should answer one question only:
What is this?
Why does it matter?
What is the most important benefit?
What proof supports it?
What should I do next?

## Typography
Use a modern, highly legible sans-serif with a premium editorial feel.
- Hero headlines should feel oversized and dominant.
- Section headlines should be clearly smaller than the hero but still visually strong.
- Body copy should be comfortable, restrained, and narrow enough to scan easily.
- Use weight, size, and spacing before adding extra colors or decorative treatments.
- Avoid long all-caps text and avoid making every sentence bold.

## Navigation
Use a thin, understated top navigation.
- Logo or wordmark on the left.
- A small set of essential navigation links.
- One clear primary action on the right.
- On scroll, a subtle translucent or solid background may appear for legibility.
- Navigation should remain visually secondary to the page content.

## Hero
The hero must communicate the product in one glance.
Include:
- Optional small eyebrow label.
- One short, high-impact headline.
- One concise supporting sentence.
- One primary call to action.
- Optional secondary text link.
- One large product, interface, or lifestyle visual.

Do not put a feature grid, testimonial carousel, multiple competing buttons, or a paragraph of copy in the hero.

## Story sections
Build the page as a sequence of large editorial stories rather than a collection of small cards.

Recommended rhythm:
- A light section introducing the core benefit.
- A large visual demonstration.
- An asymmetric text-and-image story.
- A dark cinematic section for the strongest or most emotional feature.
- A restrained proof section using a few high-value facts, outcomes, or testimonials.
- A final action section that feels like the natural conclusion of the story.

Every story section must have one primary message.

## Feature presentation
If multiple features must be shown, group them into a small number of substantial panels rather than many tiny cards.
Each panel should contain:
- A short label.
- A strong headline.
- One sentence of explanation.
- One meaningful visual.

Panels may use soft backgrounds and generous internal spacing. Borders and shadows should be subtle or absent.

## Imagery
Treat imagery as a primary storytelling tool.
Use one strong visual whenever possible instead of several weak visuals.
Good options include:
- Large product close-ups.
- Device or interface mockups.
- Full-width lifestyle photography.
- Cropped product details.
- A product emerging from a dark background.
- Subtle perspective views.

Images should feel intentional, high quality, and uncluttered.

## Motion
Motion is optional and must remain restrained.
Good uses:
- Gentle fade or rise on entry.
- Slow image scale.
- Subtle parallax.
- Sticky storytelling where the visual changes as the user scrolls.
- Smooth transitions between product states.

Do not use bouncy motion, constant animation, spinning objects, or effects that distract from reading.

## Calls to action
Use one visually dominant primary action per major decision point.
Secondary actions should be quieter, often as text links.
Do not create several equally loud buttons in the same section.

## Mobile behavior
The mobile design must feel intentionally composed rather than compressed from desktop.
- Preserve strong headline hierarchy.
- Stack content into a simple single-column flow.
- Keep images large and immersive.
- Maintain generous whitespace.
- Keep tap targets obvious.
- Remove decorative elements before shrinking essential content.

## Footer
Use a quiet, structured footer with essential navigation, support, legal, and company links.
The footer should feel useful but visually subordinate to the rest of the page.

## Mandatory page structure
Use this order unless the product itself makes one section unnecessary:
Hero
Core benefit
Visual demonstration
Feature story
Dark cinematic story
Proof
Final call to action
Footer

## Optional enhancements
Only add these if they strengthen the story without increasing cognitive load:
- Sticky scroll storytelling.
- Very subtle background transitions.
- A restrained product comparison.
- A short testimonial.
- A compact proof statistic.
- A secondary product view.

## Final instruction to the design AI
Do not design a generic startup landing page. Design a premium product story with disciplined hierarchy, generous whitespace, minimal copy, strong imagery, and one clear idea per section. When forced to choose between adding another element and preserving clarity, preserve clarity.
